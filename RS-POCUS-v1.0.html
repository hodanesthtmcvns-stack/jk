1. Indication & Clinical Context
1b. Suggested Sequence, Probe Principle & Core Objectives
Recommended principle: use the curvilinear/microconvex probe as a versatile first-line probe when it provides adequate image quality. Do not sacrifice diagnostic quality simply to maintain a single-probe workflow — switch to a phased-array or linear probe when it materially improves the required examination.

Core objectives

  • Identify immediately important obstructive causes: tamponade, tension pneumothorax and major RV pressure overload/possible PE.
  • Characterize gross LV and RV systolic function and chamber relationships.
  • Look for pulmonary interstitial syndrome, pleural effusion, consolidation and pneumothorax-related signs.
  • Assess venous filling/IVC behavior as one contextual variable rather than a stand-alone volume or fluid-responsiveness test.
  • When a fluid challenge is being considered, determine whether a reversible preload challenge produces a meaningful increase in forward flow and whether the lungs appear tolerant of additional fluid.
  • Repeat POCUS after major therapeutic interventions when the clinical question persists.
Evidence status: this app implements a focused POCUS teaching protocol revised against PubMed-indexed studies, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, peer-reviewed full texts and the 2024 SCCM focused update on adult critical care ultrasonography. It deliberately avoids treating single ultrasound measurements as stand-alone determinants of fluid administration or shock diagnosis.
2. Step 1 — Subcostal Cardiac Window 60–90 s

Probe position: subxiphoid/subcostal, using the liver as an acoustic window. Obtain a subcostal four-chamber view when possible.

2b. Teaching Notices
Important correction: RV/LV enlargement should not be assigned a universal threshold such as RV:LV >0.6 for diagnosis of massive PE. RV enlargement is a non-specific sign and should be integrated with clinical probability, venous findings, lung ultrasound and other investigations.
D-sign teaching point: systolic septal flattening is more consistent with RV pressure overload, whereas diastolic flattening is more consistent with RV volume overload. Mixed flattening may occur with combined loading conditions.
3. Step 2 — IVC Assessment 30–45 s

Context, not a stand-alone fluid test. Obtain a longitudinal subcostal IVC view with the vessel entering the right atrium; measure perpendicular to its long axis at a consistent site. A high-quality B-mode cine loop is often preferable to M-mode alone for confirming anatomy and avoiding off-axis measurements.

Confounders present

3b. Evidence Note
The 2.1-cm / 50% collapsibility framework is primarily an estimate of right atrial pressure in appropriate echocardiographic contexts; it should not be converted directly into a universal fluid-responsiveness rule. In spontaneously breathing patients, IVC collapsibility has only moderate diagnostic accuracy and should not be used in isolation.

Major confounders include spontaneous respiratory effort, mechanical ventilation settings, right-heart failure, tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension, tamponade, intra-abdominal hypertension, elevated PEEP, severe COPD/auto-PEEP and altered venous compliance. When these are present, prioritize dynamic flow-based assessment when feasible.

4. Step 3 — Lung Ultrasound 60 s

Use the same curvilinear/microconvex probe when image quality is adequate. A limited screen is appropriate during immediate resuscitation; extend posteriorly and complete the examination when the diagnosis remains uncertain.

Aggregate lung pattern and pneumothorax/effusion/consolidation flags are computed live in the decision-support cards above.

4b. Teaching Notices
B-lines are not synonymous with cardiogenic pulmonary edema. Diffuse B-lines indicate an interstitial-alveolar syndrome and can occur in cardiogenic edema, ARDS, pneumonia and other conditions. Absence of lung sliding should not be documented as "pneumothorax" unless the overall examination (lung point, pleural-line assessment, clinical setting) supports that diagnosis.
5. Step 4 — Abdominal / Vascular Screen 30–60 s, if indicated

Perform FAST views, abdominal aortic screening or focused venous assessment only when clinically indicated. The curvilinear probe is well suited to these deeper examinations.

5b. Interpretive Cautions
A negative FAST does not exclude all internal bleeding. A focused DVT examination can support PE evaluation but does not by itself establish PE. Do not use IVC appearance alone to diagnose fluid overload or obstruction.
6. Step 5 — Fluid Responsiveness: PLR + LVOT VTI Variable

Fluid responsiveness means an increase in cardiac output or stroke volume after a preload increase — it does not mean the patient requires fluid.

PLR/LVOT VTI interpretation is computed live in the decision-support cards above.

6b. Technical Requirement & Evidence
Technical requirement: if a reliable LVOT VTI cannot be obtained with the curvilinear probe, do not manufacture a numerical result — use another validated flow-based method or a different transducer if available.
PLR coupled to a flow variable (cardiac output or its direct surrogate) has strong diagnostic performance — pooled sensitivity ~86%, specificity ~92% in meta-analysis — and performs better than pulse-pressure changes. A reproducible increase in VTI of approximately 10–15% is commonly used as supportive evidence of preload responsiveness, though thresholds vary by technique and clinical context.

Core teaching principle: fluid responsiveness ≠ fluid requirement. A patient can be preload responsive while simultaneously being at high risk of harm from additional fluid. POCUS should generate a working physiological phenotype, not a single-label diagnosis — mixed shock is common.
7c. Evidence-Based Teaching Pearls & Limitations
  • A curvilinear/microconvex probe is a versatile shock POCUS probe, not a universal replacement for every ultrasound transducer.
  • IVC size and respiratory variation are contextual findings; do not use them as an isolated prescription for fluid.
  • PLR coupled to a flow variable is substantially more robust than static volume surrogates for testing preload responsiveness.
  • B-lines indicate an interstitial syndrome; they do not by themselves diagnose cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
  • Absent lung sliding is not synonymous with pneumothorax; look for a coherent pleural pattern and, when possible, a lung point.
  • RV enlargement is not synonymous with acute PE and should be integrated with the clinical probability and other POCUS findings.
  • Fluid responsiveness is a physiological property. Fluid requirement is a treatment decision.
  • Serial, integrated reassessment is more defensible than a one-time threshold-driven decision.

Limitations

  • Probe: less optimal cardiac temporal/superficial resolution than a phased-array probe; less suitable for superficial structures than a linear probe.
  • Window: severe obesity, bowel gas, abdominal distension, dressings, subcutaneous emphysema and prior surgery may prevent adequate subcostal imaging.
  • IVC: diameter and respiratory variation are strongly affected by ventilation, respiratory effort, right-heart pathology and intra-abdominal pressure.
  • Lung: B-lines and absent lung sliding are pattern findings, not single-diagnosis findings.
  • Operator dependence: all focused ultrasound findings depend on acquisition quality and interpretation. Residents should obtain supervised competency before using quantitative measurements for management.
  • Doppler: VTI is highly dependent on alignment, sample location and beat selection. Poor alignment should be reported as a limitation rather than converted into a false numerical value.
  • PE: RV enlargement is supportive but non-specific. POCUS cannot reliably exclude PE in isolation.
  • Tamponade: pericardial effusion is not synonymous with tamponade. Chamber collapse and hemodynamic context matter.
  • Time: ≈5 minutes is a training target for a core screen, not a validated universal completion time.

About RS-POCUS

RS-POCUS (Rapid Shock POCUS) v1.0 Curvilinear/microconvex-probe protocol for undifferentiated shock: phenotyping, fluid responsiveness and fluid tolerance. Purpose: Structured bedside documentation and lightweight decision support for the five-step curvilinear-probe shock POCUS sequence — subcostal cardiac, IVC, lung, abdominal/vascular, and PLR/LVOT VTI — for emergency medicine and critical care residents. Design principle: The operator remains the author of the scan and its interpretation. The software provides standard terminology, structured documentation, and evidence-anchored teaching captions without imposing unnecessary clerical burden. No shock phenotype or fluid decision should be triggered from a single isolated finding. This tool supports documentation and teaching. It does not replace formal echocardiography, comprehensive diagnostic ultrasound, CT, specialist consultation, established resuscitation protocols, or consultant judgement.
